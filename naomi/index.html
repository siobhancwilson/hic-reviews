What is Naomi?

Naomi is a statistical model that maps South Africa's HIV epidemic district by district. It was developed at Imperial College London with UNAIDS, and South Africa uses it for the district estimates in its annual UNAIDS submission.

Think of it as a high-resolution snapshot. Where the Thembisa model tells the national story over time, Naomi takes many imperfect data sources — household surveys, antenatal testing, and treatment programme data — and combines them to answer one question: where is HIV right now, and where is the need greatest?

It produces estimates for all 52 districts, broken down by 5-year age group and sex, every estimate calibrated to add up to Thembisa's provincial totals.

Two maps in one: prevalence vs burden

The highest prevalence is rural and eastern — Umkhanyakude DM (27.0%), Zululand DM (25.1%) and the rest of northern KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga. But the greatest number of people living with HIV sits in the big metros, where populations are largest: Johannesburg MM, Ekurhuleni MM and Tshwane MM together hold far more PLHIV than any rural district. Prevalence tells you where the epidemic is most intense; counts tell you where the most people need services.

Who is most affected?

Naomi breaks every district down by age and sex — revealing who carries the burden

Among adults, women are far more affected: about 5.17M women (15+) live with HIV, compared with 2.63M men — women are 66% of adults living with HIV. A further 129k children (under 15) are living with HIV.

People living with HIV by age & sex, Sept 2026

Women living with HIV peak in their late 30s and 40s — and women are affected far younger than men

Why the age & sex pattern matters

The bulge of women in their 30s and 40s reflects infections acquired years earlier, now living long lives on treatment. The earlier rise among women than men — visible in the younger bands — is why adolescent girls and young women remain a prevention priority. Naomi's age×sex detail in every district is what lets programmes target the right people in the right place.

Watch the catchment trap: people travel for treatment

Naomi reports ART two ways — by where patients live (residents) and where they collect treatment (attending). The two diverge sharply: Ehlanzeni DM treats about 47,228 more people than live there, while G Sibande DM sends roughly 44,947 residents elsewhere for care. Coverage measured at the facility can flatter urban districts and understate the rural districts that send patients to them — always check whether a coverage figure is resident-based or attendance-based.

Treatment is now the main prevention tool

New infections (~132,000/yr) are a fraction of the 7.93M already living with HIV. Because a person on effective treatment does not transmit, closing the treatment gap — especially in the high-incidence, high-unmet-need districts — is the most direct way to drive new infections down further.

Why antenatal data is so valuable

Almost all pregnant women attend a clinic and are routinely tested, so ANC gives a near-complete, repeated read on HIV in a key population — making it one of the strongest signals Naomi uses to estimate prevalence and ART coverage in every district. High prior-ART coverage at first visit (80%) also shows how far prevention of mother-to-child transmission has come.

The big picture

What Naomi reveals — the wins, the gaps, and where to push next

South Africa's epidemic is enormous but increasingly well-managed: 96% of people with HIV know their status and 80.2% are on treatment. Naomi's value is showing that these national averages hide wide district-level inequality — and pointing precisely to where the remaining 1.57M people not on treatment actually are.

Biggest successes

  • First 95 essentially met — 96% of PLHIV know their status nationally.
  • 80.2% on treatment — one of the largest ART programmes in the world.
  • Strong PMTCT — 80% of HIV-positive pregnant women already on ART at first ANC visit.
  • District × age × sex detail — resources can be aimed, not sprayed.

Biggest gaps

  • 1.57M not on treatment — concentrated in the metros (Johannesburg 219k, Ekurhuleni 149k).
  • Coverage inequality — from 66% in N Mandela Bay MM up to the high 80s elsewhere.
  • Prevalence hotspots — up to 27.0% of adults in Umkhanyakude DM.
  • Women and young people — women are 66% of adults with HIV, affected younger than men.

Where to act

  • Target the highest unmet-need districts — the metros hold the most people off treatment.
  • Lift the lowest-coverage districts toward the national 80%.
  • Account for patient travel when planning facility capacity (residents vs attending).
  • Reach women and young people with testing, treatment and prevention where Naomi shows the burden is youngest.

How does it work?

Combining many imperfect sources into one picture

Naomi is a data-synthesis model — it does not simulate transmission, it triangulates the best estimate from the evidence we have

Borrowing strength across districts

No single data source is complete in every district. Naomi uses Bayesian spatial smoothing — where a small district has little survey data, the model learns from its neighbours to produce a stable estimate. It then splits each estimate by age and sex, and finally calibrates every district so the totals match Thembisa's provincial numbers.

What data does it use?

  • National household HIV surveys (prevalence, ART, by age/sex)
  • Antenatal clinic HIV testing data
  • ART programme numbers from health facilities
  • Stats SA population estimates (the denominators)

Calibrated to Thembisa

Naomi's district estimates are constrained to add up to Thembisa's provincial totals. If Thembisa says a province has a certain number of people living with HIV, that province's districts in Naomi must sum to it — keeping the two models consistent.

Things to know

What makes Naomi special

Why it is the tool of choice for sub-national HIV planning

District-level detail

The only model that systematically produces HIV estimates for every district by age and sex — the resolution programmes actually plan at.

Uncertainty quantified

Every estimate comes with a credible interval, so you can see where the model is confident and where data is thin.

Triangulates many sources

Combines surveys, antenatal testing and programme data into one coherent picture rather than relying on any single source.

Built for action

Answers “where is the need right now?” to guide resource allocation — complementing Thembisa's “how did we get here?”

Using these statistics — important notes

How to read, quote and compare the numbers in this guide

A snapshot, not a trend. Naomi tells you where things stand at a point in time (plus a short projection). It cannot show how the epidemic evolved over decades — for long-run trends use Thembisa.

These are modelled estimates, with uncertainty. Every figure is an estimate, not a headcount, and carries a credible interval. Where survey data is sparse, intervals widen — check them before quoting a district number as fact.

Residents vs attending. ART can be counted by where people live or where they collect treatment. The two differ sharply in districts that send or receive patients — always check which basis a coverage figure uses.

Denominators drive the rates. Prevalence and coverage depend on Stats SA population estimates. If a district's population is off, its rates will be too, even when the PLHIV count is right.

Use the mean. Naomi outputs mean, median and mode; official reporting (and this guide) uses the mean. For small, skewed districts the mean and median can differ.

Tied to a vintage. Each run uses specific input data and a calibration date. This guide uses the December 2025 run, Thembisa-calibrated, and shows the September 2026 estimate. Don't compare figures across runs without checking what changed. This run excludes viral-load suppression, so the third 95 is not shown.

How to cite Naomi

When quoting Naomi outputs, cite the methods paper describing the model:

Eaton JW, et al. (2021) Naomi: a new modelling tool for estimating HIV epidemic indicators at the district level in sub-Saharan Africa. Journal of the International AIDS Society. 24(S5): e25788.

Remember that estimates carry credible intervals, that this is a December 2025 run showing a September 2026 projection, and that district figures are calibrated to add up to Thembisa's provincial totals.
