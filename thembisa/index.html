What is Thembisa?

Thembisa is a mathematical model of South Africa's HIV epidemic, developed at the University of Cape Town. The name means "give hope" in isiXhosa and isiZulu.

Think of it as a movie of the epidemic. It reconstructs everything that has happened with HIV in South Africa since the first infections in 1985, simulates the present, and projects forward into the 2030s. Month by month, it tracks how people get infected, start treatment, and what happens to them over time.

Since 2017, Thembisa has been the official basis for South Africa's UNAIDS HIV estimates.

South Africa has one of the largest HIV epidemics in the world. Roughly 1 in 8 people are living with HIV. Women are disproportionately affected — approximately 5.2 million women (15+) compared with 2.6 million men.

Who is most affected?

National averages hide huge differences. Two things vary across groups — who is most at risk, and who is hardest to reach.

Who carries the most HIV — prevalence by group, 2026

Key populations — sex workers, transgender women, people who inject drugs, and men who have sex with men — live with HIV at three to four times the national rate. Among adults, women (21%) carry roughly double the burden of men (12%), and new infections concentrate in the young: adolescent girls and young women see the most new infections of any group — about 40,000 a year, roughly 30% of all new infections. They are both biologically more susceptible to HIV and more likely to have older partners, which is why prevention efforts single them out.
But being most affected isn't only about risk — it's also about reach. Men are less likely to stay on treatment once diagnosed (77% on ART vs 87% of women), and people who inject drugs are the least reached of all (40% on ART, 8% on PrEP).

Is it getting better?

Where — and among whom — new infections concentrate

HIV incidence by group (new infections / year)

New infections are far from evenly spread. Among people who inject drugs, incidence (~16% a year) runs roughly 66× the general rate, and young women remain the single largest group of new infections. Provincially, incidence is highest where prevalence is highest.

New infections peaked in 1999 at about 545,000 a year — nearly 1,500 a day — and have fallen by roughly three-quarters since, driven by the rollout of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and prevention programmes. But today's ~134,000 a year — about 370 people infected every day — is still far too high.

★ Are we winning? One number to watch

The clearest single sign of progress is the epidemic-control ratio — new infections measured against the number of people already living with HIV. For every 100 people living with HIV in South Africa, about 1.7 are newly infected each year (a ratio of 0.017).

UNAIDS treats an epidemic as being brought under control once this ratio drops below 0.03. South Africa is now comfortably under that line — and so is every single province (KwaZulu-Natal is doing best at 0.013; the Eastern Cape is furthest off at 0.026). In plain terms: treatment is finally outpacing new infections.

What is the human cost?

The toll HIV has taken on lives, and how treatment is changing the picture

Life expectancy at birth

HIV shortened lives dramatically — ART has brought a remarkable recovery

Child survival

HIV drove child deaths up through the 2000s; ART and the prevention of mother-to-child transmission have reversed it. Under-5 mortality has fallen from about 86 to 36 per 1000 live births, and infant mortality from about 58 to 26.

Two numbers capture both the toll and the turnaround: AIDS-related deaths have fallen to about 52,000 a year — down from a peak near 269,000 in 2005 — while life expectancy has climbed back to about 66 years after sinking to 54 in 2004. Many of the remaining deaths are preventable — they occur in people who were diagnosed but never started treatment, or who stopped and returned to care too late, with advanced disease. Tuberculosis remains the leading cause of death among people living with HIV, which is why HIV and TB programmes work hand in hand.

Are people getting treated?

How far has South Africa come in getting people onto antiretroviral therapy (ART)?

Testing — the front door to the cascade

The cascade starts with a test. About 88% of adults have ever tested for HIV — up from just 50% in 2010 — though men (85%) still lag women (92%).

The gap is the middle 95 — getting diagnosed people onto treatment. About 83% of those who know their status are on ART, short of the 95% target; roughly 1.3 million people know their status yet are not on treatment. Progress that was on track until about 2020 stalled through the COVID years, leaving the country roughly a million people short of where the treatment target should be. The government's Close The Gap campaign (2025) is aimed squarely here.

The treatment journey, 2002–2026

Every person with HIV, by treatment status — the undiagnosed share shrinks as treatment scales up

In a quarter-century the undiagnosed share fell from ~80% to ~4%, and treatment now reaches ~80% of everyone with HIV. The remaining work is the rest — people diagnosed but not yet on treatment, or who have stopped after starting.

Starting, stopping and restarting

Treatment isn't one-and-done. Only about 161,000 people now start ART for the first time each year, but around 679,000 restart after an interruption — four times as many. About 756,000 people who once started ART are currently off it, and about 293,000 of those restarting come back with advanced disease (CD4 <200). Keeping people in care — not just getting them started — is now the central challenge, and the focus of the Close The Gap campaign.

KwaZulu-Natal leads throughout. The widest gaps are getting people onto treatment (Limpopo & Gauteng 77%) and population-level suppression — just 76% of everyone with HIV is virally suppressed, the truest measure of treatment's impact.

How early do people start treatment?

When ART began, most people started desperately late. The share starting at CD4 <200 (advanced disease) has fallen from about 91% in 2002 to 24% in 2026 — people now start far earlier, when treatment works best.

Preventing new infections

Treatment keeps people alive; prevention stops new infections in the first place. The main tools all vary widely by province.

Medical male circumcision

About 68% of men 15–49 are circumcised, roughly halving female-to-male transmission — from 93% in Limpopo to 44% in the Northern Cape.

Condom use

About 27% of young women (15–24) used a condom at last sex (women's reported use). Use is low and has plateaued.

PrEP — the prevention pill & injection

Pre-exposure prophylaxis reaches only about 2.9% of sexually active people overall — rising fast and heavily concentrated in Key populations (31% of sex workers, 25% of MSM). Of the roughly 396,000 people on PrEP, most are women (258,000 vs 138,000 men).

Preventing mother-to-child transmission

About 1 in 5 pregnant women is living with HIV (about 19%, rising with age from ~6% under 20 to over 40% past 40), so preventing transmission to their babies is critical. About 98% are on ART at delivery, cutting vertical transmission to about 2.4% — one of South Africa's biggest successes. New infections in children have dropped about 12-fold in two decades — from roughly 230 a day in the mid-2000s to about 19 a day now. But most remaining infant infections now happen during breastfeeding, to mothers who were never diagnosed — many of whom acquire HIV during pregnancy or breastfeeding themselves (an incidence of about 0.7% a year).

Why prevention still matters

Even with treatment now outpacing new infections, around 134,000 people are still infected each year. Prevention — condoms, PrEP, circumcision, and treating people so they cannot transmit the virus — is what will eventually drive that number toward zero.

The big picture

What jumps out — the wins, the gaps, and where to push next

South Africa runs the world's largest HIV programme, and the direction is unmistakably positive: deaths and new infections are now a fraction of their peaks, and for the first time treatment is outpacing new infections. But the epidemic is far from over — the hard part has shifted from starting people on treatment to keeping them in care, and from the general population to the groups hardest to reach.

Biggest successes

  • Deaths down ~80% from the 2005 peak; life expectancy up 12 years (54 → 66).
  • New infections down ~75%, and the epidemic-control ratio (0.017) sits below the 0.03 line in every province.
  • 96% know their status — the first 95 is essentially met.
  • Mother-to-child transmission cut to 2.4%; child infections down ~12-fold.

Biggest gaps

  • The middle 95: only 83% of diagnosed people are on ART — ~1.3 million know their status but aren't treated, and progress stalled after 2020, ~a million short of target.
  • Retention, not starts: 679k restart after interrupting (4× first-time starts), 756k are currently off ART, and population-level suppression is just 76%.
  • Hardest-hit, least-reached: people who inject drugs (16% incidence, 40% on ART, 8% PrEP), young women (30% of new infections), and men (lagging on testing, ART and circumcision). PrEP reaches just 2.9%.

Where to focus

  • Keep people in care — re-engaging those who have stopped is the single highest-leverage gap (Close The Gap, 2025).
  • Close the middle 95 — get the 1.3 million diagnosed-but-untreated onto ART.
  • Reach men, young women and people who inject drugs with testing, treatment and prevention.
  • Scale PrEP from a low base, focused where risk is highest.

What's making headlines

The numbers behind the news: the treatment drive is “off track by about a million” after a COVID-era slowdown; teen girls and young women remain a policy priority; and treatment-as-prevention (a virally suppressed person cannot transmit HIV) is why getting — and keeping — people on ART is now the country's main prevention tool.

How does it work?

A virtual copy of the population

Thembisa creates a simplified version of everyone in South Africa and simulates what happens to them over time

How the simulation works

Imagine sorting every person in South Africa into groups by age, sex, risk level, and HIV status. Each month, the model moves people between groups — some get infected, some start treatment, some die. Run this forward from 1985 and you get the full story of the epidemic.

What data does it use?

  • National household HIV surveys
  • HIV testing data from antenatal clinics
  • Death records from the Department of Home Affairs
  • Treatment programme numbers from health facilities

Why we can trust it

The model is tuned until it matches real-world data. If the simulation produces a number for AIDS deaths in a given year, that number has to line up with what death records actually show. This calibration process is what makes the estimates credible.

Using these statistics — important notes

How to read, quote and compare the numbers in this guide

These are modelled estimates, with uncertainty. Every figure is a model estimate, not a headcount, and comes with a 95% confidence interval — check how wide it is before quoting a number as fact. The intervals reflect uncertainty in HIV transmission, disease progression, mortality and testing, but not demographic parameters (so population size and infant mortality carry extra uncertainty) or future intervention rollout.

Estimates vs projections. Estimates are fitted to historic data; projections (recent and future years) extrapolate past trends with no confirming data, assuming current programmes and behaviour continue — they show what would happen if trends hold, not what will happen if policies change. Treat projected figures, including the recent years shown throughout this guide, with caution.

Timing. Stock variables (e.g. people living with HIV, ART coverage) are reported at the middle of each year; flow variables (e.g. new infections, AIDS deaths, births) are totalled over the 12 months from mid-year to mid of the next year.

National, provincial & district. National figures come from a separate national calibration, not by summing the provinces, so provincial totals will not add up exactly to the national figure (the national/SA figures are the more reliable source). Thembisa only goes down to provincial level — for district estimates use the Naomi model (Imperial College London), whose district figures are calibrated to add up to Thembisa's provincial totals.

Don't mix versions. Each version (v4.8, then 5.0) updates both data and methodology, so comparing numbers across versions is misleading.

Terminology. “Male/female” means sex at birth, not gender identity; transgender women are reported but transgender men are not modelled. “Adults” = 15 and older, “children” = under 15. “Clients of sex workers” = men who had sex with a female sex worker in the last 12 months. “Sexually active” = had sex in the last 12 months. “Vertical transmission” = mother-to-child transmission. An “ART interruption” = at least one month off ART after having started.

How to cite Thembisa

When quoting Thembisa outputs, the model's authors ask that you cite the most recent journal publication describing the model:

Johnson LF, et al. (2022) The effect of HIV programs in South Africa on national HIV incidence trends, 2000–2019. Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes. 90: 115–123.

Remember that estimates carry 95% confidence intervals, and that figures for recent years are projections, not data-confirmed estimates. National figures are taken from the model's national calibration — not by summing the provinces.
